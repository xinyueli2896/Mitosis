\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Generation across modalities is posed at one of two distances. Vision
and language lie far apart: neither the form nor the content of one
signal is recoverable from the other, and the bridge between them has
to be learned as an alignment~\cite{clip,flamingo}. Text and speech lie
near: they carry the same content in different form, and the bridge is
a translation~\cite{tts,asr}. At either distance the field has, until
recently, posed generation as conditioning, one signal given the other
in full: captioning and text-to-image at the far distance, speech
synthesis and recognition at the near one. Conditioning fixes a
direction at the moment of the split, and the signal that is given is
never written with the generated one in view. That is now changing.
Models that produce both signals at once, speech together with its
text~\cite{moshi}, sound together with video~\cite{mmdiffusion}, are
the first in which the two signals unfold side by side and neither is
finished before the other begins.

Joint production raises a question that conditioning never faces.
Each signal follows its own grammar, and a network that models both
must give each some computation of its own; yet the two signals are
not independent, and at every instant what one does must fit what the
other is doing. Separation serves the first demand and sharing serves
the second. Existing joint models resolve the tension by design rather
than by learning. One line shares every parameter and tells the
signals apart only by a type token~\cite{chameleon,moshi}, or by
modality-specific input and output layers around a shared
trunk~\cite{transfusion}; the other gives each modality its own
feed-forward and attention weights and shares only the attention
computation~\cite{mot,bagel}, or assigns each modality its own group
of experts inside the trunk~\cite{moma}. In all of these, where the
computation of one signal should diverge from the other's is fixed
before training starts.

Whether that choice is right cannot be tested where it is made.
Paired data across modalities is scarce relative to data in either
modality alone, so every cross-modal model starts from networks
pretrained on one modality and trains only the bridge on pairs. The
natural control, a single model trained from scratch on the paired
data with everything shared, does not exist, because the data to train
it does not exist. Any comparison between a shared and a partitioned
design is therefore confounded with what each inherited from
pretraining, and the question of how much two signals should share
has never been asked against its own null hypothesis.

We ask it where the null hypothesis can be built. Our two signals are
two \strms{} of one modality: melody and chords in symbolic music. A
modality is a signal fixed by its representation; a \strm{} co-occurs
with another inside that representation, sharing its vocabulary and
its clock yet obeying its own grammar. Melody and chords have the
property that makes two modalities interesting, distinct content under
distinct rules: a melody is governed by contour, phrase and step
motion, a chord track by voice leading and harmonic rhythm. What they
lack is the property that gets in the way: they need no translation,
since both are note events on one grid. Two consequences follow.
Paired data is abundant, so the fully shared generator, one sequence
model over the merged note stream~\cite{cp_transformer,compose_embellish,whole_song},
is not a hypothetical control but the established design, and it
becomes the yardstick every other design has to beat or explain. And
because the two \strms{} share one representation, any specialisation
a model learns is specialisation by grammar: in a vision--language
mixture, modality-specific experts would separate on pixels against
words before any grammar entered, whereas here there is nothing to
separate on but the music. The result is not confined to music.
Cross-modal pairs reach the same condition once both signals are
tokenised into one vocabulary and interleaved on one
clock~\cite{moshi,audiopalm}; past the point of alignment, they are
two \strms{}, and what holds here holds for them.

Within symbolic music, generators of melody and chords fall into the
same two lines as their cross-modal counterparts, and each gives up
one side of the question. Lead-sheet models merge the two \strms{}
into a single token sequence in which a note's \strm{} is a token
attribute and every parameter is shared~\cite{compose_embellish,mmm,whole_song};
the \strm{}'s grammar has nowhere of its own to form. Harmonisation
and chord-conditioned melody models condition one \strm{} on the other
given in full~\cite{yeh2021,sun2021,surprisenet}; the direction is
fixed and the given \strm{} was never written with its partner in
view. Neither keeps the \strms{} distinct while letting them shape each
other as they unfold.

We propose a duet transformer that does both. It is a single
autoregressive model over the two \strms{} interleaved frame by frame,
warm-started from a pretrained single-\strm{} generator. Two
components are \strm{}-specific: the attention projections, and a
router over a shared pool of feed-forward experts. The pool is
unassigned. No expert belongs to a \strm{} in advance; which experts
serve melody, which serve chords and which serve both is left to the
routers to learn, which is what separates this design from mixtures
that partition experts by modality a priori~\cite{moma}. To let the
\strms{} agree within a frame and not only across frames, we append
query slots, one per \strm{}, trained as masked predictors of the
current frame under random commitment of either partner and decoded by
iterative refinement or by committing one \strm{} and conditioning the
other. One checkpoint therefore generates both \strms{} together, or
either given the other, with no change of architecture.
