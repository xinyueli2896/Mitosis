\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Generation across modalities is posed at one of two distances: vision
and language lie far apart and the bridge is learned as an
alignment~\cite{clip,flamingo}; text and speech lie near and the
bridge is a translation~\cite{tts,asr}. At either distance most
systems produce one modality at a time: one signal given the other in
full, a cascade of such stages, or a unified model that writes the
modalities segment by segment into one
sequence~\cite{chameleon,transfusion,ualm,bagel}. A smaller line
produces two at once. Token streams advance in lock-step with a fixed
order inside each step, as the text token that precedes each frame of
speech in Moshi~\cite{moshi} or the delayed codebooks of
MusicGen~\cite{musicgen}; joint diffusion denoises both signals with
no order at all~\cite{mmdiffusion}; and any-to-any or guidance methods
couple two generators through a shared latent~\cite{codi,seeing_hearing},
so that neither reads the other while it is written.

Producing two signals at once raises a question conditioning never
faces: each signal follows its own grammar and needs computation of
its own, yet at every instant what one does must fit what the other is
doing. Existing designs settle the split in advance, sharing every
weight but a type token or the input and output
layers~\cite{chameleon,transfusion,ualm,moshi}, or giving each
modality, or each token role, its own attention and feed-forward
weights~\cite{mot,bagel} or experts~\cite{moma}. Neither
choice can be tested where it is made: paired data across modalities
is scarce, so such models start from single-modality pretraining and
train only the bridge on pairs; the control, everything shared and
trained from scratch on the pairs, has no data to train on.

We ask the question where the control can be built: two streams of
one modality, melody and chords in symbolic music. A stream shares its
partner's representation and clock but not its grammar, so the pair
has distinct content under distinct rules without needing translation:
both are note events on one grid. Paired data is abundant, and the
fully shared generator, one sequence model over the merged note
stream~\cite{cp_transformer,compose_embellish,whole_song}, is the
established design and the yardstick every other must beat or explain.
Specialisation learned here is specialisation by grammar, the
condition cross-modal pairs reach once tokenised into one vocabulary
on one clock~\cite{moshi,audiopalm}.

Within music, lead-sheet models share every parameter over one merged
sequence~\cite{compose_embellish,mmm,whole_song} and harmonisation
models give one stream in full~\cite{yeh2021,sun2021,surprisenet};
neither keeps the
streams distinct while letting them shape each other as they unfold.
Our duet transformer does: one autoregressive model over the streams
interleaved frame by frame, stream-specific in its attention
projections and in a router over a shared, unassigned pool of
feed-forward experts, so which experts serve melody, chords or both is
learned rather than imposed~\cite{moma}. Query slots, one per stream,
are trained as masked predictors of the current frame under random
commitment of either partner; at decode the leader is committed and
the follower conditioned on it, alternating frame by frame where the
lock-step models fix the order for good, so each stream reads the
other's current frame equally often.
