\subsection{Listening test}
\label{sec:listening}

Objective metrics say how far a continuation sits from its reference;
they do not say whether it is music. We therefore ran a listening test
on the co-generation systems of Sec.~\ref{sec:systems}, with the
ground-truth continuation as a reference item. Five held-out POP909
songs were used, each cropped as in Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}; for every
song, each system's continuation and the ground truth were rendered
with the same piano and string timbres for melody and chord, so that
the clips differed only in the notes [X: clip length; prompt heard
first; wording of the page]. A rater was assigned a song and heard all
six clips of it in random order, rating each on five axes from $1$ to
$5$: \emph{consistency} (does the continuation follow on from the
prompt), \emph{structure} (is there phrasing and development over the
clip rather than drift or repetition), \emph{melody--chord fit} (do the
two streams belong together), \emph{musicality} and \emph{creativity}.
Raters could take further songs.

Excluding a pilot on an earlier version of the page, 21 people rated
438 clips [X: recruitment]. We discarded four raters who gave one identical score to
every clip on every axis, three of them ten years old and none with
musical training; their removal changes no significance result below,
since a constant rater contributes only ties. The remaining 17 raters
(10 male, 7 female; ages 21--55, median 27) had \emph{some} musical
training in 11 cases, \emph{none} in 4, and 2 were amateur musicians.
They produced 372 ratings, 62 per system, between 6 and 30 per rater
(median 30), and every song was rated by at least eleven raters.
Because each rater heard every system on a song, a rater--song pair is
a paired unit: we compare each system with \sys on the 62 pairs with
a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, per axis and on the mean over
axes.

Table~\ref{tab:listening} gives the result. \sys is the highest-rated
system on four of the five axes and overall ($3.81$), and the
differences fall into three groups. Against the single-stream model
trained from scratch ($2.99$) and Whole-Song-Gen ($3.35$) it is
preferred on every axis ($p \le 0.04$ throughout, $p < 0.001$
overall), by $0.83$ and $0.46$ points on average; the scratch model is
rated lower in 47 of 62 pairs and higher in 10. Against the
Anticipatory Music Transformer ($3.65$) the margin is $0.16$ overall
and not significant ($p = 0.12$); the one axis that separates them is
consistency with the prompt ($+0.31$, $p = 0.03$), the axis on which a
general-purpose model trained on a different corpus would be expected
to give ground. Against the single-stream model finetuned on POP909
($3.71$) there is no difference on any axis: \sys leads by $0.10$
overall ($p = 0.33$) and trails by $0.13$ on musicality ($p = 0.37$).
Ground truth remains clearly above every system ($4.24$, $p \le 0.04$
on every axis against \sys); the gap is smallest on consistency
($0.26$) and largest on musicality ($0.61$).

Two readings follow. First, the ordering of the listening test agrees
with the objective metrics of Table~\ref{tab:e1}: the systems that sit
farthest from the reference distributions are the ones raters marked
down, and the two matched single-stream models bracket \sys from
below, the finetuned one closely. Second, the margin of \sys over
the two external systems is largest on consistency with the prompt
($0.66$ and $0.31$) and smallest on musicality and creativity ($0.39$
and $0.31$ against Whole-Song-Gen, $0.05$ on both against the
Anticipatory Music Transformer), so what separates the systems in the
ear is how the continuation relates to what came before rather than
how good its material is on its own. This is what the two-stream
construction is meant to buy: the same backbone, held to a shared
clock and reading its partner, keeps the continuation attached to the
prompt and the two streams attached to each other. What it does not
buy is better material than a single-stream model of the same size
finetuned on the same songs, and we do not claim it.
